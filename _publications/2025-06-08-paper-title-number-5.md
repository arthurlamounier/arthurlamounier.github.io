Decades of fire exclusion in the southeastern U.S. has altered forest structure and composition, threatening fire-dependent oak (Quercus spp.) and pine (Pinus spp.) forests. To mimic the effects of demographic and structural changes associated with fire exclusion, we examined how the gradual replacement of longleaf pine (P. palustris) leaf litter by sweetgum (Liquidambar styraciflua) interacts with canopy cover to influence flammability and seedling survival and growth of several species with different fire and shade tolerances: shortleaf pine (P. echinata), white oak (Q. alba)/northern red oak (Q. rubra), sweetgum, and red maple (Acer rubrum). In January 2020, we hand-planted seedlings of each species across a canopy cover gradient at two sites in the southeastern United States. One year later, plots received litter fuelbed treatments with varying contributions (10-100%) of longleaf pine and sweetgum litter, while maintaining a 30% contribution of southern red oak (Q. falcata). Flammability declined with increasing sweetgum litter, and increasing canopy cover reduced flammability more in 100% Sweetgum fuelbeds than other fuelbed types. Fuelbeds with < 30% pine litter had significantly reduced flammability compared to treatments with 60-100% Pine. Across species, seedling survival and growth were greatest in unburned or 100% Sweetgum treatments. Increasing canopy cover above 55% reduced oak growth without affecting survival and suppressed red maple growth despite benefiting its survival. Our findings show that replacing pine with sweetgum litter reduces flammability, particularly as canopy cover increases, and that 30% pine contribution to the fuelbed is a potential threshold for maintaining flammability within pine-hardwood fuelbeds.
